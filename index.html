Geologic Time

Unit 1

The Concept of Time Scales in Arts & Sciences

Every discipline organizes time around major turning points—known as marker events—rather than arbitrary tick marks on a clock.

How Different Fields Divide Time

Imagine trying to describe 4.6 billion years without chapters. Just as a book uses chapters to mark shifts in a story, scientists and historians divide time whenever something fundamental changes.

The Historian’s Time Scale

Historians divide human prehistory and history by technological breakthroughs that transformed how people lived: the Stone Age, the Bronze Age, and the Iron Age. More recent history is similarly marked by cultural, political, or technological milestones—from ancient empires to the Industrial Revolution and the digital era.

The Astronomer’s Time Scale

Astronomers zoom out even further—measuring billions of years from the Big Bang (~13.8 billion years ago) through the formation of the first stars, galaxies, our Solar System, and the Sun’s eventual evolution. Again, each boundary is defined by a major physical marker event.

Unit 2

How Is Geologic Time Measured?

In geology, the primary marker events are biological: the appearance (birth) of new species or their mass extinction.

Fossils as Timekeepers & the Unit Ma

Because evolution never repeats itself in exactly the same way, each fossil assemblage acts like a unique timestamp in the rock record. Geologists measure these vast spans in mega-annums, abbreviated Ma (1 Ma = 1,000,000 years), and giga-annums, abbreviated Ga (1 Ga = 1,000,000,000 years).

“Our knowledge of organic remains teaches us, that species have a definite existence, and a centralization in geological time as well as in geographical space, and that no species is repeated in time.” — Edward Forbes, The Natural History of the European Seas

Before diving into the timeline, keep three big benchmark questions in mind: How old is the universe? (~13.8 billion years) · How old is the Earth? (~4.6 billion years, or 4,600 Ma) · How old is visible life on Earth? (~542 Ma, marking the start of the Phanerozoic Eon).

Architecture of the Geologic Time Scale

Geologic time is organized hierarchically from broadest to finest: Supereon → Eon → Era → Period → Epoch.

  • Precambrian Supereon (4,600–542 Ma): Encompasses the Hadean, Archean, and Proterozoic Eons. Covers nearly 88% of Earth’s history, before abundant hard-shelled visible life appeared.
  • Phanerozoic Eon (542–0 Ma): The age of “visible life,” divided into three Eras—Paleozoic (542–251 Ma), Mesozoic (251–66 Ma), and Cenozoic (66 Ma–Present).
  • Mass Extinctions: The end of each Era is punctuated by a catastrophic mass extinction event, resetting the ecological stage.
  • Periods: Each Era is subdivided into Periods defined by the emergence or dominance of signature organisms.
Unit 3 · 4,600–542 Ma

The Precambrian Supereon

The longest chapter of Earth’s story—spanning nearly four billion years before skeletons, shells, or forests existed.

Precambrian Supereon (Hadean, Archean & Proterozoic Eons)

~4,600 – 542 Ma
Subdivisions Hadean (4.6–3.8 Ga), Archean (3.8–2.5 Ga), Proterozoic (2.5–0.542 Ga)
Marker Event No major forms of visible life; bacteria & first multicellular organisms only

Earth formed around 4,600 Ma (4.6 Ga) during the fiery Hadean Eon (“hell”), cooled to form its first crust and oceans, and hosted the earliest single-celled bacteria and algae in the Archean Eon (“ancient”). By the Proterozoic Eon (“before life”), oxygen began accumulating and the very first soft-bodied multicellular organisms appeared—leaving behind rare fossils because they lacked hard shells or bones.

Unit 4 · 542–251 Ma

The Paleozoic Era (“Ancient Life”)

Life explodes in the oceans, colonizes land with vascular plants and insects, develops backbones and jaws, and ends in Earth’s largest mass extinction.

Cambrian Period — Beginning of the Paleozoic Era

~542 – 488 Ma
Name Origin Cambria (Roman name for Wales, Great Britain)
Marker Event Explosion of visible, hard-shelled life on Earth

Suddenly in the rock record, marine ecosystems burst with diversity—an event famous as the Cambrian Explosion. Organisms evolved hard shells and exoskeletons, dramatically increasing their odds of fossilization. Iconic trilobites crawled across shallow seafloors, becoming one of the hallmark index fossils of the early Paleozoic.

Ordovician Period

~488 – 443 Ma
Name Origin Ordovices (an ancient Celtic tribe in Wales)
Marker Event First jawless fish (earliest vertebrates)

Invertebrates still ruled the oceans—trilobites reached peak diversity alongside corals and brachiopods—but a quiet revolution was underway: the appearance of the first jawless fish, our earliest vertebrate ancestors, armored in bony plates.

Silurian Period

443 – 416 Ma
Name Origin Silures (a Celtic tribe of the Welsh borderlands)
Marker Event Vascular plants appear on land

For billions of years, Earth’s continents were barren rock. During the Silurian, vascular plants—plants with internal plumbing to transport water and nutrients—took root along shorelines and wetlands, paving the way for terrestrial ecosystems.

Devonian Period

416 – 359 Ma
Name Origin Devon, England
Marker Event Age of Fishes

Known worldwide as the Age of Fishes, Devonian seas hosted everything from armored placoderms and early sharks to lobe-finned fishes—the group that would eventually evolve limbs and crawl onto land.

Carboniferous Period (Mississippian & Pennsylvanian)

~359 – 299 Ma
Name Origin Latin for “coal-bearing”
Marker Events Towering trees dominate, first amphibians & first reptiles, vast coal swamps

In North America, geologists split the Carboniferous into two sub-periods—the Mississippian and the Pennsylvanian—reflecting a dramatic shift from shallow tropical seas to lush, coal-forming swamps.

Mississippian Time (~359–318 Ma)

Named for rocks exposed along the Mississippi Valley, Mississippian time saw extremely high global sea levels and the rise of the first amphibians. Much of eastern North America lay beneath a warm, shallow inland sea—depositing thick layers of fossil-rich marine limestone.

Right Here in Tennessee: Celina, TN You can see the Mississippian Period in person near Celina, Tennessee, where thick outcrops of Mississippian marine limestone record that ancient shallow sea.

Pennsylvanian Time (~318–299 Ma)

Named after the state of Pennsylvania, where extensive coal beds of this age are mined, the Pennsylvanian was marked by vast coal-forming swamps, abundant sharks in coastal waters, and a remarkable variety of insects (including giant dragonflies fueled by high atmospheric oxygen).

Permian Period — End of the Paleozoic Era

299 – 251 Ma
Name Origin Permia, Russia
Key Tectonic & Climate Events Coal swamps diminish; Appalachian Orogeny; Pangaea assembled

As continents collided to form the supercontinent Pangaea—triggering the Appalachian Orogeny that built the ancient Appalachian Mountains—interior climates dried out and coal-forming swamps shrank.

Major Mass Extinction #1 (The Largest in Earth History) At the end of the Permian (~251 Ma), widespread volcanism triggered catastrophic climate and ocean disruption: ~90% of marine species and ~70% of land species went extinct, closing the Paleozoic Era.
Unit 5 · 251–66 Ma

The Mesozoic Era (“Middle Life”)

The Age of Reptiles and Dinosaurs—also witnessing the first mammals, the first flowering plants, and a dramatic asteroid-driven finale.

Triassic Period — Beginning of the Mesozoic Era

~251 – 200 Ma
Name Origin Trias (three distinct rock units in Germany)
Marker Event Dinosaurs appear

In the wake of the Permian extinction, ecosystems slowly recovered and diversified. Reptiles radiated into vacant niches, and the first dinosaurs appeared on Pangaea.

Jurassic Period

201 – 145 Ma
Name Origin Jura Mountains (Alps, Western Europe)
Marker Events Age of Reptiles; First Mammals appear

As Pangaea began to rift apart, giant dinosaurs dominated land, pterosaurs patrolled the skies, and marine reptiles ruled the seas. Tucked quietly into the undergrowth were the first mammals—small, mostly nocturnal creatures living in the shadow of giants.

Cretaceous Period — End of the Mesozoic Era

145 – 66 Ma
Name Origin Creta (Latin for chalk — chalk deposits were widespread)
Marker Events First placental mammals & early flowering plants appear

Warm seas covered broad continental shelves, depositing thick blankets of microscopic marine shells that hardened into chalk—giving the Cretaceous its name. On land, early flowering plants transformed landscapes and first placental mammals appeared.

Major Mass Extinction #2 (66 Ma) The Mesozoic Era ended abruptly when a massive asteroid impact struck Earth, triggering global wildfires, impact winter, and ecological collapse—wiping out all non-avian dinosaurs.
Unit 6 · 66 Ma–Present

The Cenozoic Era (“Recent Life”)

With dinosaurs gone, mammals rapidly diversify to fill land, sea, and air—leading through ice ages to the rise of humans.

Paleogene Period — Cenozoic Era Begins

66 – 23 Ma
Name Origin Greek for “ancient-born”
Marker Event Age of Mammals begins

Following the asteroid impact, surviving small mammals exploded in size and diversity, launching the Age of Mammals across forests and grasslands.

Neogene Period

23 – 2.6 Ma
Marker Events First hominids appear; mammoths dominant

As global climates cooled and grasslands expanded, giant grazing mammals such as mammoths thrived, and the first hominids appeared in Africa.

Quaternary Period

2.6 – 0 Ma (Present)
Marker Events Last Ice Age & Our Times

The Quaternary spans repeated glacial cycles—including the Last Ice Age, when woolly mammoths roamed across frozen steppes—and brings us right into the present day (the Holocene Epoch).

Geologic Time in Perspective: The Cosmic Calendar

To grasp just how vast 13.8 billion years of cosmic history—and 4.6 billion years of Earth history—really are, imagine compressing the entire history of the universe into a single calendar year. The Big Bang happens at midnight on January 1. Our Solar System doesn’t form until September. Visible life explodes in mid-December, dinosaurs appear on December 25 and vanish on December 30, and all of recorded human history happens in the final seconds before midnight on December 31.
